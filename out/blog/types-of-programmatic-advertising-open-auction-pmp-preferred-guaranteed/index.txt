15:T79cd,<p data-pm-slice="1 1 []">Programmatic advertising is not a single buying method. Behind the word &ldquo;programmatic&rdquo; sits a group of transaction models that determine <strong>who can buy inventory, how the price is set, whether impressions are reserved, and how much control buyers and publishers have over the deal</strong>.</p>
<p>For advertisers in Bangladesh, that distinction matters.</p>
<p>A Dhaka-based e-commerce brand that wants wide reach at competitive prices may have very different media-buying requirements from a bank launching a premium credit card, a telecom operator promoting a national campaign, or an international advertiser trying to secure inventory on selected Bangladeshi publishers.</p>
<p>Those advertisers may all use programmatic technology, but they should not necessarily buy media in the same way.</p>
<p>The four models most marketers need to understand are:</p>
<ul data-spread="false">
<li>
<p>Open Auction</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Private Marketplace (PMP)</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Preferred Deal</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Programmatic Guaranteed</p>
</li>
</ul>
<p>Although these options share the same broader programmatic ecosystem, they differ significantly in access, pricing, priority, inventory assurance, transparency, and scale.</p>
<p>This guide explains those differences from a practical buying perspective, with particular attention to advertisers, agencies, and publishers working in the <strong>Bangladesh digital advertising market</strong>.</p>
<div><hr></div>
<h2>Programmatic Deal Types at a Glance</h2>
<p>Before examining each model individually, it helps to see the basic differences.</p>
<table>
<tbody>
<tr>
<th>Deal Type</th>
<th>Who Can Buy?</th>
<th>Pricing</th>
<th>Inventory Reserved?</th>
<th>Typical Use</th>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>Open Auction</td>
<td>Broad pool of eligible buyers</td>
<td>Auction-based</td>
<td>No</td>
<td>Scale and flexible buying</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>Private Marketplace / Private Auction</td>
<td>Invited buyers</td>
<td>Auction with floor price</td>
<td>No</td>
<td>Controlled access to selected inventory</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>Preferred Deal</td>
<td>Specific buyer</td>
<td>Negotiated fixed price</td>
<td>No</td>
<td>First-look access without commitment</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>Programmatic Guaranteed</td>
<td>One negotiated buyer</td>
<td>Fixed negotiated price</td>
<td>Yes</td>
<td>Reserved premium inventory</td>
</tr>
</tbody>
</table>
<p>Google Ad Manager classifies Open Auction as inventory available to all eligible buyers, while Preferred Deals and Private Auctions are forms of Private Marketplace transactions. Programmatic Guaranteed, by contrast, involves inventory reserved for a specific buyer under negotiated terms.</p>
<p>The most important distinction is not simply &ldquo;public versus private.&rdquo;</p>
<p>It is the combination of:</p>
<p><strong>access + price mechanism + reservation + commitment.</strong></p>
<h1>1. Open Auction: Maximum Access and Competitive Bidding</h1>
<p>An <strong>Open Auction</strong> is the broadest programmatic buying environment.</p>
<p>Publisher inventory is made available to a wide pool of eligible buyers. Advertisers or their DSPs evaluate impressions and bid according to their campaign rules.</p>
<p>Google describes an Open Auction as an auction-based transaction where inventory is available to all eligible buyers.</p>
<p>This model is sometimes associated with terms such as:</p>
<ul data-spread="false">
<li>
<p>Open exchange</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Open marketplace</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>RTB marketplace</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Public auction</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Exchange buying</p>
</li>
</ul>
<p>The exact terminology varies by platform, but the underlying idea is similar: multiple buyers can compete for available impressions.</p>
<h2>How an Open Auction Works</h2>
<p>A simplified transaction might look like this:</p>
<ol start="1" data-spread="false">
<li>
<p>A user opens a webpage or app.</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>An advertising opportunity becomes available.</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>The publisher's monetization technology makes the impression eligible for programmatic demand.</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Buyers evaluate the impression.</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Eligible buyers submit bids.</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>The platform determines the winning bid according to its auction rules.</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>The winning creative is served.</p>
</li>
</ol>
<p>In Google Ad Manager's Open Auction and Private Auction environment, the winner is determined using the highest qualifying net bid or expected value under its auction logic.</p>
<p><img src="https://api.boostrava.com/assets/images/blog/content/2026/08/33f473eb-35d6-4bb1-9074-bff1b8df84b5.webp" alt="" width="1920" height="1080"></p>
<p>For advertisers, the practical takeaway is simple:</p>
<p><strong>Open Auction provides flexibility and scale, but you usually have less certainty about individual inventory availability than in a reserved deal.</strong></p>
<div><hr></div>
<h2>Why Bangladesh Advertisers May Use Open Auctions</h2>
<p>Open Auctions can be useful when the campaign needs broad delivery rather than exclusive access to a specific publisher.</p>
<p>For example, imagine a Bangladeshi online retailer promoting an Eid campaign.</p>
<p>Its objectives may include:</p>
<ul data-spread="false">
<li>
<p>Reaching users across Dhaka, Chattogram, Sylhet, Rajshahi, and other markets</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Buying across multiple websites and apps</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Testing several audience segments</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Comparing mobile and desktop performance</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Optimizing toward conversions</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Scaling spend where performance is strongest</p>
</li>
</ul>
<p>The advertiser may not care whether every impression appears on one specific publisher.</p>
<p>Instead, it cares about whether the impression meets criteria such as:</p>
<ul data-spread="false">
<li>
<p>Geography</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Device</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Context</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Audience</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Bid price</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Brand-safety settings</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Campaign objective</p>
</li>
</ul>
<p>In that situation, Open Auction buying can offer significant flexibility.</p>
<div><hr></div>
<h2>Advantages of Open Auction</h2>
<h3>Broad Inventory Access</h3>
<p>Advertisers can potentially compete for impressions across many publishers and inventory sources.</p>
<h3>Flexible Budgets</h3>
<p>Campaigns can often be increased, reduced, paused, or redirected without the type of fixed delivery commitment found in guaranteed deals.</p>
<h3>Real-Time Price Competition</h3>
<p>Advertisers bid according to the value they assign to each available impression.</p>
<h3>Useful for Testing</h3>
<p>New advertisers can test:</p>
<ul data-spread="false">
<li>
<p>Targeting strategies</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Creative messages</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Regions</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Devices</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Publisher categories</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Conversion performance</p>
</li>
</ul>
<p>before committing larger budgets to specific inventory.</p>
<h3>Scalable Performance Buying</h3>
<p>For advertisers focused on measurable outcomes, Open Auction buying can provide a large pool of impressions that optimization systems can evaluate.</p>
<div><hr></div>
<h2>Limitations of Open Auction</h2>
<p>The same openness that creates scale also creates trade-offs.</p>
<p>Advertisers may have:</p>
<ul data-spread="false">
<li>
<p>Less certainty about specific inventory</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Less control over premium placement availability</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>More variation in publisher quality</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Greater need for exclusions and verification</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>More competition for desirable impressions</p>
</li>
</ul>
<p>Publishers may also prefer not to expose every premium placement to a broad marketplace.</p>
<p>That leads to private deal structures.</p>
<div><hr></div>
<h1>2. Private Marketplace (PMP): Controlled Programmatic Access</h1>
<p>A <strong>Private Marketplace</strong>, commonly called a <strong>PMP</strong>, creates a more controlled buying environment.</p>
<p>Instead of allowing a broad pool of buyers to compete, the publisher limits access to selected advertisers, agencies, or demand partners.</p>
<p>In Google Ad Manager terminology, Preferred Deals and Private Auctions are both considered PMP deal types.</p>
<p>This is important because marketers sometimes use &ldquo;PMP&rdquo; as though it were one single transaction model.</p>
<p>It is better to think of PMP as a <strong>private access category</strong> that can contain different deal structures.</p>
<p>One common PMP structure is a Private Auction.</p>
<div><hr></div>
<h2>How a Private Auction Works</h2>
<p>A publisher identifies a group of approved buyers.</p>
<p>Those buyers receive access to selected inventory.</p>
<p>They then compete within that restricted auction environment.</p>
<p>Unlike a Preferred Deal, where a specific price is negotiated with a buyer, a Private Auction normally uses a <strong>floor price</strong> and competitive bidding among invited buyers.</p>
<p>Google describes Private Auctions as non-guaranteed PMP transactions where specific allowlisted buyers participate in an auction, while the publisher establishes a floor price.</p>
<div><hr></div>
<h2>Bangladesh Example: Premium News Inventory</h2>
<p>Suppose a major Bangladeshi publisher has premium homepage, business-news, and sports inventory.</p>
<p>The publisher may not want every programmatic buyer to access certain placements through the open market.</p>
<p>Instead, it could invite:</p>
<ul data-spread="false">
<li>
<p>Major banks</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Telecom companies</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>FMCG brands</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Large e-commerce companies</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Selected agencies</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>International brands targeting Bangladesh</p>
</li>
</ul>
<p>into a private marketplace.</p>
<p>The publisher maintains tighter control over who gets access, while advertisers gain a clearer path to higher-value inventory.</p>
<div><hr></div>
<h2>Why Advertisers Choose PMP Deals</h2>
<h3>Better Inventory Control</h3>
<p>Advertisers can gain access to inventory that may not be broadly available in Open Auction.</p>
<h3>Stronger Publisher Relationships</h3>
<p>PMP buying can combine automated transactions with direct commercial relationships.</p>
<h3>Greater Supply Transparency</h3>
<p>Depending on the platform and agreement, buyers may have clearer visibility into publisher identity and inventory source.</p>
<h3>More Brand-Sensitive Environments</h3>
<p>Advertisers in regulated or reputation-sensitive industries may value tighter inventory controls.</p>
<p>For example, a bank, insurance company, healthcare advertiser, or financial services brand may prefer selected publisher environments over unrestricted scale.</p>
<h3>Access to Premium Audiences or Sections</h3>
<p>Publishers may package:</p>
<ul data-spread="false">
<li>
<p>Finance content</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Sports content</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Technology sections</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Premium mobile inventory</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>High-impact placements</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Video inventory</p>
</li>
</ul>
<p>for private buyers.</p>
<div><hr></div>
<h2>PMP Does Not Mean Guaranteed</h2>
<p>This is one of the most important concepts to understand.</p>
<p>A PMP deal does <strong>not automatically guarantee delivery</strong>.</p>
<p>Private Auction deals are typically non-guaranteed.</p>
<p>Advertisers receive access to the inventory but still need to participate according to the deal rules.</p>
<p>That makes PMP different from Programmatic Guaranteed.</p>
<div><hr></div>
<h1>3. Preferred Deals: First Opportunity at a Fixed Price</h1>
<p>A <strong>Preferred Deal</strong> sits between open bidding and guaranteed reservation.</p>
<p>The publisher and buyer negotiate:</p>
<ul data-spread="false">
<li>
<p>Specific inventory</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Commercial terms</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>A fixed CPM or agreed price</p>
</li>
</ul>
<p>The buyer then receives a preferred opportunity to purchase qualifying inventory before it flows into other eligible auction environments.</p>
<p>However, the inventory is <strong>not reserved</strong>.</p>
<p>Google describes Preferred Deals as fixed-price, pre-auction transactions negotiated with specific buyers. The buyer has the preferred opportunity to buy the inventory, but is not obligated to purchase it, and the publisher does not reserve it for that buyer.</p>
<p>This combination creates flexibility for both sides.</p>
<div><hr></div>
<h2>What &ldquo;Preferred&rdquo; Actually Means</h2>
<p>Imagine a publisher and advertiser agree on a CPM of $4 for selected inventory.</p>
<p>When an eligible impression becomes available, the preferred buyer gets an early opportunity to buy it under the negotiated terms.</p>
<p>If the buyer does not take the impression, that inventory can potentially continue to other eligible demand, including private or open auction environments depending on the publisher's setup.</p>
<p>The key characteristics are:</p>
<p><strong>negotiated access + fixed price + no guaranteed volume.</strong></p>
<div><hr></div>
<h2>Bangladesh Example: Travel Campaign</h2>
<p>Imagine a travel company wants to advertise holiday packages to Bangladeshi travelers.</p>
<p>The company identifies a publisher with strong travel, lifestyle, and international news readership.</p>
<p>The advertiser likes the publisher but does not want to commit to purchasing a fixed quantity of impressions.</p>
<p>A Preferred Deal could allow the advertiser to negotiate access to selected inventory at an agreed price while keeping purchasing flexible.</p>
<p>This may be useful when:</p>
<ul data-spread="false">
<li>
<p>Demand can fluctuate</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Campaign budgets change</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Performance needs to be evaluated continuously</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>The advertiser wants premium access without a full reservation commitment</p>
</li>
</ul>
<div><hr></div>
<h2>Advantages of Preferred Deals</h2>
<h3>Fixed Price Predictability</h3>
<p>The advertiser knows the negotiated rate.</p>
<h3>Early Access</h3>
<p>The buyer can receive a preferred opportunity before the inventory reaches broader auction demand.</p>
<h3>No Mandatory Impression Commitment</h3>
<p>The advertiser is not required to buy every available impression.</p>
<h3>Selected Inventory</h3>
<p>The deal can focus on specific inventory valuable to the advertiser.</p>
<h3>Useful Middle Ground</h3>
<p>Preferred Deals provide more control than Open Auction without requiring the commitment of Programmatic Guaranteed.</p>
<div><hr></div>
<h2>The Main Limitation</h2>
<p>The word &ldquo;preferred&rdquo; should not be confused with &ldquo;reserved.&rdquo;</p>
<p>If the buyer needs an exact or committed volume of impressions, a Preferred Deal may not be sufficient.</p>
<p>That requirement points toward Programmatic Guaranteed.</p>
<div><hr></div>
<h1>4. Programmatic Guaranteed: Reserved Inventory with Automated Execution</h1>
<p><strong>Programmatic Guaranteed</strong> combines direct deal certainty with programmatic technology.</p>
<p>The buyer and publisher agree on:</p>
<ul data-spread="false">
<li>
<p>Inventory</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Price</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Campaign dates</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Impression quantity or delivery terms</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Targeting or placement conditions</p>
</li>
</ul>
<p>The publisher then reserves that inventory for the buyer.</p>
<p>Google defines Programmatic Guaranteed as a fixed-price transaction involving a pre-committed set of inventory negotiated with a single buyer.</p>
<p>It further distinguishes Programmatic Guaranteed from Preferred Deals by explaining that guaranteed inventory is reserved for the buyer, whereas Preferred Deal inventory is not.</p>
<p>That reservation is the core difference.</p>
<div><hr></div>
<h2>Bangladesh Example: Major Brand Launch</h2>
<p>Suppose a smartphone manufacturer plans a nationwide product launch in Bangladesh.</p>
<p>The brand wants premium placements during launch week and cannot risk discovering that the required inventory has already been sold.</p>
<p>It may negotiate a Programmatic Guaranteed deal covering:</p>
<ul data-spread="false">
<li>
<p>Selected premium publishers</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Specific dates</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Homepage or high-impact inventory</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Agreed impression volume</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Fixed CPM</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Approved creative formats</p>
</li>
</ul>
<p>Instead of buying the placement through a manual insertion order and operating the campaign completely outside <a href="https://www.boostrava.com/blog/programmatic-advertising-for-beginners-a-practical-first-campaign-guide/">programmatic infrastructure</a>, the parties can automate portions of transaction management and delivery.</p>
<p>The advertiser gets inventory certainty.</p>
<p>The publisher gets a committed buyer.</p>
<div><hr></div>
<h2>When Programmatic Guaranteed Makes Sense</h2>
<p>This deal type is particularly relevant when an advertiser needs:</p>
<h3>Guaranteed Delivery</h3>
<p>The campaign requires a defined inventory commitment.</p>
<h3>Premium Placement</h3>
<p>Specific inventory is strategically important.</p>
<h3>Fixed Campaign Dates</h3>
<p>For example:</p>
<ul data-spread="false">
<li>
<p>Eid campaigns</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Ramadan promotions</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Product launches</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Major sporting events</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>National promotional periods</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Financial product launches</p>
</li>
</ul>
<h3>Predictable Pricing</h3>
<p>The negotiated price is established in advance.</p>
<h3>Direct Publisher Relationship</h3>
<p>The buyer wants the benefits of a negotiated media deal while maintaining programmatic workflow efficiencies.</p>

<h1>The Programmatic Deal Spectrum</h1>
<p>Rather than thinking about these models as competing products, consider them a spectrum.</p>
<h3>More Open and Flexible</h3>

<p>&darr;</p>

<p><strong>Private Marketplace / Private Auction</strong></p>

<h3>Negotiated Buyer Priority</h3>
<p><strong>Preferred Deal</strong></p>
<p>&darr;</p>
<h3>Maximum Delivery Certainty</h3>
<p><strong>Programmatic Guaranteed</strong></p>
<p>As you move down the spectrum, advertisers generally gain more certainty and publisher control, while giving up some of the flexibility associated with an open marketplace.</p>
<p>That is why the &ldquo;best&rdquo; transaction type depends on the campaign.</p>

<h1>Pricing: Why the Lowest CPM Is Not Always the Best Deal</h1>
<p>Advertisers often compare deal types by CPM.</p>
<p>That is useful, but incomplete.</p>
<p>Consider two hypothetical choices:</p>
<h3>Option A</h3>
<p>Open Auction CPM: $0.80</p>
<h3>Option B</h3>
<p>PMP CPM: $2.50</p>
<p>At first glance, Option A looks far cheaper.</p>
<p>But what if the PMP inventory delivers:</p>
<ul data-spread="false">
<li>
<p>Better visibility</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>More relevant audiences</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Stronger publisher environments</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Higher engagement</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Better conversion quality</p>
</li>
</ul>
<p>Then the more expensive CPM might deliver a lower acquisition cost or stronger business outcome.</p>
<p>The correct question is not:</p>
<p><strong>&ldquo;Which deal type gives me the cheapest CPM?&rdquo;</strong></p>
<p>It is:</p>
<p><strong>&ldquo;Which transaction gives me the best value for the campaign objective?&rdquo;</strong></p>

<h1>Deal IDs and Why They Matter</h1>
<p>Private <a href="https://oaaa.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/10/ProgrammaticOOH_Webinar0917_PT2.pdf">programmatic transactions</a> commonly rely on a <strong>deal ID</strong>.</p>
<p>A deal ID identifies negotiated terms between the buyer and seller so participating platforms know which impressions and rules belong to that agreement.</p>
<p>Google notes that Preferred Deals, Private Auctions, Programmatic Guaranteed transactions, and related marketplace deal types are managed through deal workflows and associated with deal IDs.</p>
<p>For an advertiser, the deal ID helps connect:</p>
<ul data-spread="false">
<li>
<p>Buyer</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Seller</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Inventory</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Pricing</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Eligibility</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Transaction rules</p>
</li>
</ul>
<p>It is essentially a technical reference for the negotiated programmatic relationship.</p>

<h1>Is RTB the Same as Open Auction?</h1>
<p>Not exactly.</p>
<p>Real-time bidding describes an automated auction process where impressions can be evaluated and bids submitted in real time.</p>
<p>Open Auction commonly uses auction-based bidding, but programmatic advertising also includes private auctions, preferred transactions, and guaranteed arrangements.</p>
<p>Therefore:</p>
<p><strong>RTB is a transaction mechanism. Programmatic advertising is the broader category.</strong></p>
<p>This distinction becomes particularly important when advertisers move from basic media buying into curated and direct programmatic deals.</p>

<p>Publishers also need a monetization strategy.</p>
<p>A publisher may decide to divide inventory into several commercial layers.</p>
<p>For example:</p>
<h3>Premium Reserved Inventory</h3>
<p>Sold through guaranteed deals.</p>
<h3>Premium Non-Guaranteed Inventory</h3>
<p>Made available through Preferred Deals or PMP arrangements.</p>
<h3>Auction Inventory</h3>
<p>Made available to broader demand through Open Auction.</p>
<p>This approach can help publishers balance:</p>
<ul data-spread="false">
<li>
<p>Revenue</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Fill rate</p>

</li>
<li>
<p>Pricing</p>
</li>
<li>
<p>Automation</p>
</li>
</ul>
<p>A publisher should not assume that sending every impression into the largest possible auction will always generate the best long-term commercial outcome.</p>
<p>Premium inventory may have greater value when packaged appropriately.</p>
<div><hr></div>
<h3>Common Mistakes When Choosing Programmatic Deal Types</h3>
<h3>Treating PMP as Guaranteed Inventory</h3>
<p>PMP access does not automatically mean impressions are reserved.</p>
<h3>Assuming Preferred Deals Guarantee Volume</h3>
<p>Preferred Deals are non-guaranteed.</p>
<p>The advertiser gets a preferred buying opportunity, not a guaranteed impression commitment.</p>
<h3>Buying Guaranteed Inventory Without Forecasting</h3>
<p>Reserved inventory should match realistic delivery expectations.</p>
<h3>Choosing Open Auction Only Because It Is Cheaper</h3>
<p>Low CPM does not automatically mean high-quality results.</p>
<h3>Using Premium Deals Without a Clear Reason</h3>
<p>A higher-cost private deal should solve a real business requirement.</p>
<h3>Ignoring the Buyer-Seller Relationship</h3>
<p>Programmatic automation does not eliminate commercial strategy.</p>
<p>Many private deals still begin with negotiation between advertisers, agencies, and publishers.</p>

<h1>A Practical Decision Framework</h1>
<p>Before selecting a programmatic transaction type, answer five questions.</p>
<h2>1. Do I Need a Specific Publisher?</h2>
<p>If no, Open Auction may provide sufficient scale.</p>
<p>If yes, continue.</p>
<h2>2. Do I Need Restricted Access to That Inventory?</h2>
<p>If yes, consider a PMP structure.</p>
<h2>3. Do I Want a Negotiated Fixed Price?</h2>
<p>If yes, a Preferred Deal or Programmatic Guaranteed arrangement may fit.</p>
<h2>4. Must the Inventory Be Reserved?</h2>
<p>If no, Preferred Deal may be enough.</p>
<p>If yes, consider Programmatic Guaranteed.</p>
<h2>5. Am I Willing to Commit to Delivery Terms?</h2>
<p>If yes, guaranteed buying becomes more appropriate.</p>
<p>This decision process prevents advertisers from selecting a deal type simply because it sounds more sophisticated.</p>
